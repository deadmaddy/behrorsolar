Why We're Testing It with a 2017 Luminous Zelio 1100

Our test inverter is a Luminous Zelio 1100/12V dating from 2017.

Unlike newer inverters with dedicated lithium battery profiles, this unit has conventional lead-acid battery selection modes.

Our tests are being conducted using grid electricity, not solar-panel charging.

Out-of-Box Voltage: 13.21V

Before connecting the battery, we measured its voltage using a digital multimeter.

Result: 13.21V DC

After connecting the battery to the Zelio, with grid power OFF and no external load, the reading remained 13.21V.

This became our initial baseline.

A 13.21V reading is plausible for a four-cell LiFePO₄ battery, but it cannot reliably indicate its remaining charge percentage because LiFePO₄ batteries have a relatively flat voltage curve across much of their usable capacity.

Initial Charging Test: Voltage Begins Rising

After switching grid power ON, we monitored the battery-terminal voltage with no external appliances connected.

Within approximately two minutes, voltage reached 13.40V.

At approximately five minutes, the reading was 13.43V.

Subsequent observations showed 13.45V and 13.55V.

After Approximately Five Hours: 14.00V

Around 3 AM on 3 November, after approximately five hours of charging, the voltage reached 14.00V.

A repeated reading showed 13.98V.

These measurements suggested that the charger was operating near 14V during that period.

The battery casing also remained cool to the touch, with no noticeable overheating reported.

However, we cannot establish a full battery charge merely from terminal voltage or casing temperature.

Charging current, battery state of charge and the manufacturer's approved charging limits remain important missing measurements.

Voltage Decreased to 13.80V While Grid Remained ON

The most interesting result came after the earlier 14.00V observations.

At approximately 4 AM, the terminal voltage measured 13.82V.

By 4:15 AM, it had decreased slightly further to 13.80V.

Throughout these observations, grid power remained ON and no external load was connected.

This indicates that the battery-terminal voltage decreased by approximately 0.20V from the highest observed reading while mains power remained available.

One possible explanation is that the Zelio transitioned from a higher-voltage charging stage toward a lower-voltage maintenance stage.

However, we cannot confirm actual float mode without charging-current data and additional testing.

The accurate finding is that the inverter exhibited a reduction in battery-terminal voltage after the earlier near-14V charging period.

Did the Zelio Consider the Battery Fully Charged?

During the early charging period, the inverter displayed an estimated 40 minutes remaining until full charge.

Later, this estimated time disappeared from its display.

This suggests that the Zelio's own charge-estimation system may have reached a condition associated with charging completion.

However, the Zelio was designed around lead-acid battery characteristics, which differ from LiFePO₄ batteries.

The disappearance of the countdown does not establish that the lithium battery was actually at 100% state of charge.

We have not yet obtained an independent battery state-of-charge reading.

Why Voltage Dropped to 13.30V When the Grid Was Switched OFF

During one of the initial observations, the battery-terminal voltage dropped immediately from approximately 13.45V with mains ON to 13.30V after grid power was switched OFF.

This is consistent with the applied charging voltage being removed.

It does not indicate that a substantial amount of stored energy disappeared instantly.

The 13.30V reading also cannot reliably be converted into a charge percentage because it was taken immediately after charging stopped.

Future testing will aim to establish the actual available battery capacity using more appropriate measurement methods.

Initial Findings: What We've Verified

Our current observations support the following conclusions:

  • The battery measured 13.21V directly out of the box.
  • The Zelio applied charging voltage to the battery during the initial test.
  • The highest observed charging voltage was 14.00V.
  • The battery's casing remained cool to the touch during later charging observations.
  • The Zelio stopped displaying its charging-time estimate.
  • Battery-terminal voltage decreased to 13.80V while grid power remained ON.

These are observations from our particular battery and inverter combination.

What Does the 14.6V Specification Mean?

One particularly useful detail is the manufacturer's stated upper voltage of 14.6V.

During our earlier charging test, the highest observed voltage from the Luminous Zelio was 14.00V.

That is below the 14.6V upper value shown in the manufacturer's specification sheet.

The recorded 14.00V is a measurement from our old inverter. The specification sheet states a 14.6V upper value; it does not specify a recommended continuous charging or float setting.

We still need confirmation of the recommended absorption voltage, maintenance or float settings, and charging termination behaviour for our exact battery revision.

Similarly, the stated 50A maximum charging current provides a reference limit, but we have not measured the actual charging current supplied by our Zelio.

Understanding the 100A BMS Rating

The TRYDAN product sheet specifies a 100A BMS and a maximum discharge current of 100A.

A battery management system is an electronic protection system that helps monitor and protect lithium battery cells against conditions such as overvoltage, undervoltage and excessive current.

The precise protection features and thresholds depend on the BMS design.

For our 12V inverter application, the 100A discharge rating is especially relevant.

When an inverter supplies appliances, it draws DC current from the battery. That current depends on the AC load, battery voltage, inverter efficiency and other operating conditions.

The inverter can also demand substantially higher current during certain appliance startup events.

A 100A BMS rating is therefore useful information, but it does not establish the permitted peak current or surge duration.

We would still need TRYDAN's detailed BMS specifications and confirmation that the inverter's maximum battery-side current, including transients, remains within the permitted limits.

Important: Our Connected Load Was Not a Measured 98W

The laptop charger is rated at a maximum output of 65W, while the phone charger is rated at a maximum output of 33W.

Their combined maximum charger-output ratings therefore total 98W.

However, this does not mean the battery supplied a constant 98W throughout the test.

Actual power consumption depends on the laptop's operating activity, the charging state of both devices, charger efficiency and inverter losses.

AC input power may also be higher than the chargers' output ratings when delivering maximum power because of conversion losses.

We did not have a plug-in AC watt-hour meter or a DC shunt monitor connected during this experiment.

Accordingly, the test should be described as a laptop-and-mobile-charger backup test with 98W combined maximum charger-output ratings, rather than a controlled 98W discharge test.

Why Did Battery Voltage Fall Initially and Then Stabilise?

Perhaps the most interesting result from this experiment was the change in battery-terminal voltage.

At the beginning of the backup test, voltage declined relatively quickly.

It fell from 13.66V at the first-minute checkpoint to 13.29V after 30 minutes.

This was a reduction of 0.37V.

However, the voltage behaved differently over the following two and a half hours.

Battery voltage after the first 30 minutes
Backup time Battery voltage

60 minutes 13.29V

180 minutes 13.27–13.28V

From the 30-minute to the 180-minute checkpoint, voltage decreased by only approximately 0.01–0.02V.

Possible Explanation: The LiFePO₄ Voltage Plateau

Lithium iron phosphate batteries are known for their comparatively flat discharge-voltage characteristics.

Across a substantial portion of their usable capacity, battery voltage may remain relatively steady even while energy is being consumed.

This differs from the familiar behaviour of many conventional lead-acid batteries, where terminal voltage often provides a more visibly changing indication of charge condition.

The initial voltage reduction in our test may also have been influenced by relaxation after charging stopped.

In other words, the early decline cannot simply be treated as energy depletion.

The later voltage stability is consistent with the relatively flat LiFePO₄ discharge plateau, although voltage readings alone do not conclusively establish the underlying cause.

Does Stable Voltage Mean the Battery Is Still Almost Full?

No such conclusion can be drawn from our measurements.

A LiFePO₄ battery can continue supplying electricity while its voltage changes very little.

Therefore, 13.27–13.28V at the three-hour checkpoint does not tell us exactly how much usable energy remains.

Without discharge-current measurements or reliable BMS state-of-charge information, it would be misleading to assign a specific remaining charge percentage.

Luminous Zelio 1100 Output: 211V AC Throughout the Measured Checkpoints

Alongside battery voltage, we monitored the inverter's AC output.

At the 1-minute, 5-minute, 30-minute, 60-minute, 120-minute and 180-minute checkpoints, the multimeter indicated 211V AC.

This is notable because the output reading did not change across these checkpoints, despite the gradual change in battery-terminal voltage.

Is 211V AC Acceptable?

India's nominal domestic electricity supply is 230V AC.

An output of 211V is below that nominal value.

Our observations show that the Zelio maintained 211V at the recorded measurement points during this particular test.

However, these measurements do not independently establish the inverter's waveform quality, voltage regulation under different loads, frequency stability or compliance with its original output specification.

A separate properly instrumented inverter-output test would be needed for those conclusions.

For now, we can accurately report that the inverter maintained a measured 211V AC output at every numerical checkpoint throughout the three-hour test.

What Did We Actually Prove in Three Hours?

The test provides useful real-world evidence, but it is important not to claim more than the measurements support.

Findings Supported by Our Test

  • The TRYDAN battery and 2017 Zelio operated together during the initial grid-powered charging observation.
  • The system entered battery-backed operation after mains power was switched OFF.
  • The connected laptop and mobile chargers were confirmed working normally during the one-hour checkpoint.
  • Grid-OFF operation continued through the 180-minute checkpoint.
  • The inverter output measured 211V AC at every numerical checkpoint.
  • Battery voltage stabilised near 13.27–13.28V during the second and third hours.

What We Cannot Confirm Yet

Actual usable battery capacity: We have not measured total energy delivered.

True charging efficiency: We have not measured the energy drawn from mains or stored in the battery.

Maximum backup runtime: The three-hour milestone was not a full discharge-capacity test.

Remaining charge percentage: Battery voltage alone is not sufficiently accurate.

Long-term battery health: Three hours of successful operation cannot establish battery lifespan or capacity retention.

These limitations are important, particularly because we want the Behror Solar review to provide evidence rather than marketing claims.

What We Need for a Proper Capacity Test

The logical next step is a more instrumented experiment.

To determine the battery's actual usable capacity, we need to measure how much energy leaves the battery during a controlled discharge.

A suitable DC battery monitor with a properly rated shunt could help measure current, accumulated ampere-hours and watt-hours.

An AC energy meter could separately quantify how much electricity the inverter delivers to the connected appliances.

This would also allow us to distinguish battery output from losses associated with DC-to-AC conversion.

A meaningful capacity test should begin from a verified charging condition and follow the battery manufacturer's approved discharge limits.

We do not recommend deliberately running an unfamiliar battery-inverter combination until the BMS forcibly disconnects.

Before higher-load or extended discharge testing, the battery's exact BMS surge rating, low-voltage limits and installation protection should be confirmed.

Our Updated Verdict After Three Hours

The second phase of our TRYDAN 100Ah LiFePO₄ battery review has produced encouraging results.

Our 2017 Luminous Zelio 1100 continued operating from the lithium battery for three hours while grid electricity remained OFF.

At the measured checkpoints, the inverter output remained at 211V AC.

The battery voltage initially decreased from 13.66V to 13.29V during the first 30 minutes, then remained almost unchanged, reaching 13.27–13.28V at both the two-hour and three-hour readings.

The later voltage behaviour is consistent with the characteristic flat discharge plateau of LiFePO₄ chemistry.

The newly obtained manufacturer specifications also identify a 50A maximum charging current and a 100A maximum discharge/BMS rating for the closely matching TTESS-4S100-V1 model.

Preliminary Assessment

Initial charging behaviour: Encouraging; full charging profile unverified.

Final Thoughts

This experiment demonstrates why real-world testing is useful.

Rather than relying only on published battery specifications, we are documenting how a modern lithium battery actually behaves when connected to an older home inverter.

The initial results are promising, but the most meaningful conclusions will come from measured discharge energy, charging current, controlled load tests and longer-term use.
